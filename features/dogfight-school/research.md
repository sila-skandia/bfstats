# Dogfight school: research notes

Background reading for the dogfight school: bot pilots that fly classic WWII
air combat maneuvers, and lessons where the player learns the counter to each
one. The tone to aim for is the History Channel's *Dogfights* (2006-2008): show
the fight from outside, say plainly who had the speed and who had the height,
and point at the one move that decided it.

Everything here is summarised in our own words. Sources are linked at the end
of each section. Where the sources disagree or were thin, the text says so.

---

## 1. The core ideas, in plain words

### Energy: speed plus height

A fighter's fuel for maneuvering is its **energy**: speed (kinetic energy) and
height (potential energy). The two convert into each other. Dive and you swap
height for speed; climb and you swap speed for height. A hard turn spends
energy, because the wing drags more when it pulls. The engine slowly adds
energy back. **Energy management** is the habit of never spending more than the
fight is worth, and of knowing at every moment whether you or the enemy has more
in the bank.

**Boyd's energy-maneuverability theory, in one paragraph.** In the 1960s John
Boyd, a USAF fighter pilot, and Thomas Christie, a mathematician, turned that
habit into a number. *Specific excess power* is how fast a plane can gain energy
at a given speed and turn: engine thrust minus drag, times speed, divided by
weight. Positive means the plane can climb or speed up while doing what it is
doing; negative means it is bleeding energy. Plot that for two aircraft on the
same chart and you can see, speed by speed, which one can out-turn, out-climb or
out-accelerate the other, and so which fight each pilot should pick. It turned
"he's more maneuverable" into "he wins below this speed, I win above it".

### Turn rate versus turn radius

- **Turn rate** is how fast the nose comes round (degrees per second). It wins
  the race to point at the other plane.
- **Turn radius** is how small the circle is. It wins when both planes are
  fighting for room in the same circle.

They are not the same. Going faster widens the circle; going slower tightens it
but swings the nose round more slowly once you are below a certain speed.

### Corner speed

**Corner speed** is the speed where a plane turns its nose fastest: the slowest
speed at which it can still pull its maximum load. Above it, the circle gets
bigger and the rate drops. Below it, the wing cannot pull as hard, so the rate
drops again. A pilot who sits near corner speed has the best turn available; a
pilot well above it can trade the extra speed for height; a pilot well below it
is running out of options.

A hard turn can be **instantaneous** (yank for the best rate now, and bleed
speed fast) or **sustained** (the best turn you can hold without losing speed or
height).

### Angle-off and aspect

**Angle-off** (or aspect) is how far round from the enemy's tail you are. Zero
degrees is sitting dead behind him; 90 degrees is looking at his side; 180 is
head-on. Low angle-off is the easy, comfortable gun shot. High angle-off means a
crossing shot with big lead, or a pass with no shot at all.

### The three pursuit curves

Where the attacker points his nose, relative to the defender, decides how the
range changes.

- **Lead pursuit**: nose ahead of the target. Range closes fast. This is the
  gun-shot position, because bullets must go where the target *will be*. Hold it
  too long and you fly past him.
- **Pure pursuit**: nose straight at the target. Range closes steadily. Feels
  natural, but against a turning target you end up chasing his tail round the
  outside of his circle.
- **Lag pursuit**: nose behind the target. Range holds or opens, you keep your
  speed, and you stay behind him while he burns energy turning. It is how you
  stay in the fight without overshooting.

### Closure and overshoot

**Closure** is how fast the gap is shrinking. Too much closure and you
**overshoot**: you fly past the defender, out in front of his guns, and the
roles swap. Most defensive moves in this document exist to cause an overshoot;
most offensive refinements exist to avoid one.

### The control zone, or "the six"

The **six o'clock** is the area behind the enemy, inside his turn. The
**control zone** is a cone behind him, close enough to shoot and slow enough not
to overshoot, where you can follow anything he does. Getting there and staying
there is the whole point of offensive BFM. "Check six" means look behind you.

### The lift vector: roll to aim the pull

A wing pulls the plane toward the top of the cockpit canopy. That pull is the
**lift vector**. So you do not "turn left": you roll until the top of the canopy
points where you want to go, then pull. Roll the lift vector above the enemy and
the pull carries you up and over (a high yo-yo); roll it below and you slice
down inside his turn (a low yo-yo); roll it straight at him and you are in pure
pursuit. For a game this is the single most teachable idea: *roll first, then
pull, and where the canopy points is where you go.*

### Offensive, defensive, neutral

- **Offensive**: you are behind him, or above with speed. He reacts to you.
- **Defensive**: he is behind you, or above you. You react to him. Goal one is
  to survive; goal two is to turn it neutral.
- **Neutral**: neither has the advantage, typically head-on or side by side.
  The next ten seconds decide who becomes offensive.

### Angles fighter versus energy fighter

- **Angles fighter (turn and burn)**: a light plane with big wings that can
  out-turn the enemy. Wins by staying in the circle and pulling its nose onto
  him. Zero, Spitfire, Yak-9, Ki-43.
- **Energy fighter (boom and zoom)**: a heavy, fast plane that dives and climbs
  better than it turns. Wins by refusing the turning fight, striking from above,
  and climbing away. Wildcat and P-40 against the Zero, Corsair, P-47, Fw 190;
  the Bf 109 and Mustang lean this way too.

The lesson every Pacific pilot learned in 1942 is the core of the school: fight
the fight your plane is good at, and refuse the one the enemy is good at.

## 2. The maneuver catalogue

Each entry: what it is, when it is used, how it is flown, what it gains or
risks, and the counter. The counter is what the player will be taught.

### Boom and zoom (hit and run, "slashing attack")

- **Situation:** offensive, from above. The energy fighter's signature.
- **How:** Start well above and behind the target, ideally out of the sun.
  Full throttle, roll the lift vector toward him and dive on his rear quarter.
  Lead him, fire one short burst at close range, do *not* stay to turn. Pull up
  past him and convert the dive speed back into height. Re-assess at the top.
- **Hartmann's cycle:** Erich Hartmann, the highest-scoring ace of the war,
  boiled it into four steps: see the enemy, decide whether a surprise attack is
  possible, attack from very close range, then break away (he later called the
  last step "reverse" or a coffee break: leave, reset, choose again). He held
  fire until the target filled the windscreen.
- **Gains:** Kills without ever entering a turning fight. Works against a more
  agile enemy, which is why it is the answer to the Zero.
- **Risks:** Missing the pass and then turning with him anyway. Diving below a
  second enemy. Pulling up too slowly so the target can climb after you.
- **Counter:** See it coming (keep checking high six). When he commits to the
  dive, turn hard *toward* him so his angle-off goes up and he gets a crossing
  shot with no time on target. Do not dive away from a diving plane; he is
  faster. After the pass, do not chase him up the zoom; extend away or climb on
  your own terms.

### Zoom climb / vertical extension

- **Situation:** offensive (to regain height after an attack) or defensive (to
  shake a slower or heavier pursuer).
- **How:** With a speed advantage, pull up into a steep or vertical climb at
  full power. The faster plane climbs higher before it runs out of speed. Near
  the top, roll and pull the nose back down onto the pursuer, who is now hanging
  slow below you.
- **Gains:** Turns speed into a height advantage the pursuer cannot match.
- **Risks:** Starting without enough speed margin, so both planes run out at the
  same height and you are the one stalled and pointing at the sky.
- **Counter:** Never follow a faster plane straight up. Break off the chase,
  keep your speed, and turn away or level off, so that when he comes down he has
  no slow target to fall on. If you are faster, follow and he stalls first.

### High yo-yo

- **Situation:** offensive, when you are going too fast and are about to
  overshoot a turning target, or cannot get your nose inside his turn.
- **How:** Ease the bank and pull up out of his turn plane. You climb, slow
  down, and his turn carries him round beneath you. Roll over the top and pull
  down into his turn, arriving behind him at a better angle. Think of it as
  parking your excess speed as height and drawing it back out.
- **Gains:** Avoids the overshoot without throwing away energy; ends in lag
  pursuit behind him.
- **Risks:** Climbing too steeply or too long, which hands him the chance to
  turn across your nose or extend away.
- **Counter:** When you see him pull up and out of your circle, ease off the
  turn (unload) and gather speed; when he commits back down, turn hard again so
  he arrives with no angle. If he goes too steep, extend away in a dive.

### Low yo-yo

- **Situation:** offensive, when you are too slow or too far behind to close the
  gap in a turn.
- **How:** Roll the nose below the target's turn plane and pull. The dive gives
  you speed and a smaller-looking arc across the inside of his turn. Once the
  range closes, pull back up to his height into a firing position.
- **Gains:** Closes range and angle.
- **Risks:** Arriving with too much speed and overshooting.
- **Counter:** Tighten your turn as he drops low: his angle-off grows and he
  will slide out in front of you, especially if he over-commits.

### Break turn

- **Situation:** defensive emergency. An enemy is behind you and closing.
- **How:** Full throttle, roll hard toward the attacker (so he crosses behind
  your tail) and pull as hard as the plane allows. You are deliberately spending
  energy to make his shot impossible.
- **Gains:** Raises the angle-off so he has a split-second crossing shot, and
  can force him to overshoot.
- **Risks:** Leaves you slow. If he does not overshoot, you are a slow plane
  with a fast plane behind you.
- **Counter:** The barrel roll attack (below), or a high yo-yo: do not try to
  match his turn, climb out of plane and come back down behind him.

### Flat scissors

- **Situation:** neutral-to-defensive, usually after a slow-speed overshoot when
  both planes end up side by side.
- **How:** Both planes reverse their turns again and again, crossing each other's
  path. Each pilot is trying to slow his forward travel more than the other, so
  the enemy slides out in front. Throttle back, pull hard, reverse each time the
  flight paths cross.
- **Gains:** The slower, better-turning plane wins.
- **Risks:** Both planes end near stall speed and low. Bailing out of a scissors
  you are losing gives the winner a free shot.
- **Counter:** If your plane turns worse, do not enter it: as soon as you see the
  first reversal, extend away with speed. If you must fight it, slow down earlier
  and reverse the moment his nose passes yours.

### Rolling scissors

- **Situation:** neutral, after a high-speed overshoot, often from above.
- **How:** The defender pulls up and rolls over the top toward the attacker; the
  attacker does the same. The two planes corkscrew around a common path, each
  trying to be the one above and behind at the top of the barrel.
- **Gains:** Kills the attacker's forward travel so he overshoots.
- **Risks:** It is very hard to leave. Mike Spick's famous joke is that the
  safest exit is a split-S and a great deal of hope.
- **Counter:** Fly your rolls with less speed so you hang above him, or refuse it
  entirely and extend if you have the faster plane.

### Barrel roll attack

- **Situation:** offensive, against a defender who has just broken hard.
- **How:** Instead of turning flat after him, pull up and roll in a spiral in
  the direction away from his break, rolling round about three quarters of a
  turn. The helix moves you out of his plane and drops you in behind him, nose
  pointed along his new direction.
- **Gains:** Converts his break into your shot without overshooting.
- **Risks:** Bleeds speed; mis-timed, it leaves you slow and above him with no
  shot.
- **Counter:** As he rolls up and over, reverse your break or unload and extend.
  He has spent energy on geometry; make him chase.

### Defensive barrel roll (high-g barrel roll)

- **Situation:** last-ditch defence, enemy close behind and in firing position.
- **How:** Pull up sharply and roll hard into a tight corkscrew, over the top or
  underneath, with full stick and rudder. Throttle back to dump speed.
- **Gains:** Your forward speed vanishes and his does not, so he overshoots and
  slides out in front, ideally into your sights.
- **Risks:** Spends almost all your energy. If he reads it and pulls up instead
  of overshooting, you are a slow target.
- **Counter:** The moment the defender's nose comes up and he rolls, chop your
  own throttle and pull up out of plane (a high yo-yo). Do not follow him into
  the roll at full speed.

### Immelmann

- **Situation:** offensive reversal; repositioning after an attack.
- **How:** From good speed, pull into a half loop. At the top, upside down,
  roll upright. You are now going the other way, higher and slower.
- **Gains:** A 180-degree turn that ends with height.
- **Risks:** Over the top you are slow and predictable. Against anyone with
  speed it is a gift, which is why it is a poor defensive move.
- **Counter:** Watch for him going vertical, keep your speed and turn to meet
  him as he comes out over the top, slow. If you have energy, climb under him.

*Note: the original WWI "Immelmann" was closer to what is now called a stall
turn or hammerhead.*

### Split-S

- **Situation:** disengaging downward, or reversing onto a target going the
  other way below you.
- **How:** Roll upside down, then pull through the bottom half of a loop. You
  come out heading the opposite way, lower and faster.
- **Gains:** A quick escape with speed; the classic way to leave a fight.
- **Risks:** Needs a lot of height. Spends height and turns, so it gives up
  energy you may need. A split-S too low ends in the ground.
- **Counter:** If you have the faster plane in a dive, follow. If not, let him go
  and keep your height: he gave it to you.

### Hammerhead (stall turn)

- **Situation:** reversing direction at the top of a vertical climb.
- **How:** Pull straight up. As speed bleeds to almost nothing, kick full
  rudder so the plane pivots on its wingtip and falls back down the way it came.
- **Gains:** The tightest 180-degree reversal there is. Points you straight down
  at whatever followed you up.
- **Risks:** At the top you are motionless, which any pursuer with speed can
  shoot.
- **Counter:** Do not follow him up. Hold off to the side and catch him on the
  pivot, when he cannot maneuver.

### Wingover

- **Situation:** neutral reversal that keeps your energy.
- **How:** Climb at a moderate angle, let the speed fall, and as it does, roll
  and let the nose fall sideways through the horizon into a gentle dive back the
  other way, arriving at your original height and speed.
- **Gains:** A tight reversal that costs little energy and, because the plane
  never stops, is harder to track than a stall turn.
- **Risks:** Still slow and predictable at the top.
- **Counter:** Same as the Immelmann: meet him at the slow point with speed.

### Lufbery circle

- **Situation:** group defence. A flight of slower or less agile planes forms a
  horizontal circle, each guarding the tail of the plane ahead.
- **How:** Everyone turns the same way in a ring. An attacker who chases one
  plane into the ring finds the next one behind him.
- **Gains:** Survival for weaker planes; the Bf 110 (with its rear gunner) and
  Soviet Il-2s used it in WWII.
- **Risks:** It goes nowhere. By WWII it was mostly a death trap against faster
  fighters attacking from above.
- **Counter:** Never join the circle. Attack it from above at speed, take one
  pass and zoom away; or wait outside it until someone breaks off.

### One-circle and two-circle fights

After a head-on pass, both planes turn. If they turn the **same way** across
the merge (both turning to meet nose to nose again), they fly **one circle** and
it is a **radius** fight: the plane with the smaller circle gets inside and
wins. Slow down, tighten up. If they turn **opposite ways** (both heading for the
other's tail), they fly **two circles** side by side and it is a **rate** fight:
the plane that swings its nose faster, near corner speed, gets round first.

- **Counter, as a teaching rule:** choose the circle that suits your plane. A
  slow, tight-turning plane (Zero, Spitfire) wants one circle. A plane that turns
  fast at speed but needs room wants two.

### Thach weave (beam defence)

- **Situation:** two-plane defence against a more agile enemy.
- **How:** Two fighters fly side by side, well apart. When an enemy settles in
  behind one of them, both turn hard *toward each other* and cross. The attacker,
  following his target, is dragged straight across the nose of the other
  fighter, who gets a head-on or close crossing shot. Then they weave back and
  repeat.
- **Origin:** Lt Cdr John "Jimmy" Thach worked it out in 1941, reportedly with
  matchsticks on his kitchen table, after reports that the Zero could out-turn
  and out-climb the Wildcat. He first used it at Midway on 4 June 1942, and
  Marines at Guadalcanal adopted it. James Flatley gave it the name.
- **Gains:** Turns two inferior planes into a trap; the attacker cannot stay on
  either tail without getting shot.
- **Risks:** Needs a disciplined partner. Japanese pilots learned to attack from
  above and break off before the weave closed.
- **Counter:** Do not chase one weaver into the crossing. Attack from high and
  fast, one slashing pass, and zoom away before the partner can swing round;
  or attack both at once with a wingman.

### Defensive spiral

- **Situation:** defensive, last resort, when both planes are slow and near
  stall.
- **How:** Drop the nose into a tight descending spiral. Cut the throttle so you
  are not running away from your own turn. Gravity keeps your speed up. Pull out
  as late as you dare.
- **Gains:** Forces an overshoot, or the attacker hits the ground first.
- **Risks:** The ground. It is a rolling scissors aimed straight down.
- **Counter:** Stay above and outside his spiral in lag, do not dive with him at
  his speed, and shoot when he levels out.

### The jink (guns defence)

- **Situation:** defensive, enemy close behind and firing.
- **How:** Random, abrupt changes: roll, pull, push, skid with rudder, change
  speed. The aim is that no two seconds look the same, so his lead is always
  wrong.
- **Gains:** Spoils his aim this second.
- **Risks:** It improves nothing about your position and costs energy. Buy time,
  then do something else.
- **Counter:** Stop chasing every twitch. Drop into lag, hold your nose on the
  average of where he is, and fire short bursts when he steadies.

### Extension / bug-out

- **Situation:** any time the fight is lost or pointless.
- **How:** Roll wings level or slightly nose-down, ease off the stick so the
  wing is not dragging, full power, and run straight away to build range. Only
  works if your plane is at least as fast as his.
- **Gains:** Lives to fight again; turns a losing fight into a reset.
- **Risks:** Running from a faster plane gives him a no-deflection shot.
- **Counter:** If you are faster, follow at full power and close. If not, let him
  go and climb: he will have to come back to you.

### Head-on pass (the merge) and the lead turn

- **Situation:** neutral, the start of almost every fight.
- **How:** The two planes close nose to nose. Snap shots are possible, but it is
  the deadliest moment for both. The **merge** is when they pass each other's
  wingline.
- **The lead turn:** Start your turn a moment *before* the merge, offset to one
  side, so that as you pass you are already turning onto his tail. Whoever
  gets their nose round first after the merge becomes the attacker.
- **Gains:** Turns a neutral pass into an offensive position.
- **Risks:** Turning too early shows him your belly and gives him a free shot;
  needs some offset between the two flight paths.
- **Counter:** Deny the offset. Turn toward his lead turn so you meet him nose
  to nose again, or pull vertical so the fight goes into the third dimension
  where his early turn does not help him.

### Throttle chop / forcing the overshoot

- **Situation:** defensive, attacker closing fast from behind.
- **How:** At the last moment, chop the throttle (and in many games, airbrake
  or reverse thrust), and pull or skid hard. You decelerate; he does not.
- **Gains:** He slides past and in front of your guns.
- **Risks:** You are now slow. If he sees it coming and pulls up (a high yo-yo),
  he is above you with energy and you have none.
- **Counter:** Watch his speed, not just his position. When the gap suddenly
  shrinks, pull up and away out of his plane immediately, lose closure in the
  vertical, and come back down behind him.

## 3. Aces, engagements and the *Dogfights* episodes

### Who is associated with what

| Maneuver or idea | WWII story to hang it on |
|---|---|
| Boom and zoom | **Erich Hartmann** (Bf 109, Eastern Front): see, decide, attack, reverse; fire from point-blank. **Chennault's Flying Tigers** (P-40 over Burma and China): climb above, dive through, keep going, never turn with the Japanese fighters. **Corsair pilots** such as Kenneth Walsh: keep the height, keep the speed, refuse slow turning fights with the Zero. |
| Pounce from below | **Saburo Sakai** (Zero) favoured coming up from under his target's blind spot; with Nishizawa and Ota he formed Lae's "clean-up trio". |
| Dive and roll away (escaping an angles fighter) | Testing of the captured **Akutan Zero** in 1942 showed its ailerons stiffened badly at high speed. American pilots learned to dive and roll hard (often said to be to the right) to shake a Zero, which also suffered engine cut-outs under negative g. Sources disagree on details; treat it as lore, not doctrine. |
| Thach weave | **Jimmy Thach** and Ensign Dibb at **Midway**, 4 June 1942; then Marine Wildcats at **Guadalcanal**. |
| Turning into the attack / head-on | **Stanley "Swede" Vejtasa** at Coral Sea, in an SBD Dauntless dive bomber, reportedly turned into each attacking Zero and came out with two or three kills (one by clipping a wing). The count is disputed. |
| Height and sun | **Douglas Bader** (RAF, Spitfire/Hurricane), the legless ace of the Battle of Britain, preached that whoever has the height controls the fight and an attack out of the sun goes unseen. |
| Go in fast, hit hard, get out | **Sailor Malan** (RAF, Spitfire), whose Ten Rules hung on squadron walls in 1940. |
| Aggression in a turning fight | **Pappy Boyington** (Corsair, VMF-214 "Black Sheep"). His squadron mixed it up freely; he is quoted brushing off talk of tactics. His Corsair pilots' real edge was height and speed. |
| Air ambush / bait | **Robin Olds**, Operation Bolo (Vietnam, 1967): F-4s flew a bomber profile to lure MiG-21s up. Later than WWII but a good "deception" lesson. |
| Lufbery circle | Bf 110 Zerstorer units and Soviet Il-2s; by WWII mostly a liability. |
| One-circle fight won by the Zero | Countless 1942 Wildcat and P-40 losses that the Thach weave and boom-and-zoom were invented to stop. |

## 4. Rules of thumb for the screen

Short enough for a loading tip or a lesson banner. Most are standard fighter
pilot sayings; a few are condensed from the lessons above.

**Energy**
- Speed is life.
- Altitude is life insurance.
- Out of energy, out of options.
- Never follow a faster plane up.
- Never dive away from a diving plane.
- Spend energy to gain position, never for nothing.
- Height you give up is height he gets.

**Sight and awareness**
- Lose sight, lose the fight.
- Check six. Then check it again.
- The one that kills you is the one you never see.
- Attack out of the sun.

**Choosing the fight**
- Fight your fight, not his.
- Never turn with a Zero. (Never turn with a better turner.)
- If it turns better, go vertical. If it climbs better, turn.
- Go in fast, hit hard, get out.
- One pass, then reset.

**Gunnery**
- Get close. Then get closer.
- Short bursts.
- Lead the target: shoot where he will be.

**Defence**
- Turn into the attack.
- Make him overshoot.
- Jinking buys time, not position.
- If the fight is lost, leave it.

**Teamwork**
- Two planes are a team; one plane is a target.
- Never chase the same plane as your wingman.
- Leave someone up high.

### Dicta Boelcke (1916), paraphrased

Oswald Boelcke wrote the first rulebook of air combat in WWI. In plain modern
terms:

1. Get the advantage before you attack; keep the sun behind you.
2. Once you start an attack, finish it.
3. Only shoot from close range, and only when he is properly in your sights.
4. Never lose sight of your enemy, and do not fall for his tricks.
5. Attack from behind.
6. If he dives on you, do not run: turn and meet him.
7. Over enemy ground, always know your way home.
8. Fight in small groups, and do not all chase the same target.

### Sailor Malan's Ten Rules (1940), the parts we could confirm

Malan's rules, issued across RAF Fighter Command in the Battle of Britain,
closely follow Boelcke: hold fire until very close; fire short bursts and only
when the sights are on; keep part of the formation high as top cover when
diving to attack; value initiative, aggression, discipline and teamwork; and his
best-known line, which works perfectly as an on-screen tip: "Go in quickly -
Punch hard - Get out!" Rules 3 to 7 were not available in the sources we could
open; check the RAF Benevolent Fund page before quoting them.

## 5. BF1942 itself: community lore, and what the engine does

### What the community left behind

Solid BF1942 dogfighting lore is thin online. Most early-2000s guides and forums
are gone, and what survives is mostly about control setup. What we found:

- **Throttle-cut turn with reverse.** An AnandTech forum player (2003) described
  the trick that matters most: cut the throttle mid-turn and, with enough
  height, pull the throttle back toward full reverse to nearly turn in place.
  This is the BF1942 version of the throttle chop, and it is far stronger than
  in a real aircraft.
- **Roll, then pull.** The same thread's advice for a sharp turn is to roll into
  the turn and then pull up, and to aim the whole aircraft at the target rather
  than the crosshair. That is the lift-vector lesson, discovered by players.
- **Immelmann and split-S.** A 2008 fan blog for the Wake Island demo teaches
  the loop, barrel roll, Immelmann and split-S, calls the split-S riskier at low
  height, and notes loops come out egg-shaped rather than round. It also notes
  that the game does no g-damage to the pilot, so hard maneuvers cost nothing
  physical.
- **Plane reputation.** StrategyWiki's vehicle page describes the Corsair as
  arguably the best fighter despite odd handling that changes with speed, the
  Spitfire as a stable gun platform that can loiter near stall speed, and the
  Yak-9 as a superb air-superiority fighter. (The page refused our fetch; this
  is from the search excerpt.)
- **Looping as the core move.** Players across the Battlefield series describe
  dogfighting as endless loops, because a loop is the tightest reversal the game
  offers. In BF2, players noted jets no longer struggled over the crest of the
  loop "like they did in 1942" (bf2s forum), which implies BF1942 props could
  struggle at the top of a loop at low throttle.
- **"Hanging on the prop" and flying backwards.** We found **no** surviving
  forum source that names either trick for BF1942. The engine, though, makes
  the first one real (see below), so it is very likely what players were doing.

### What the engine does that changes the lessons

> **Overruled by measurement (2026-11-07).** The list below was written from
> the flight-model notes' older sections (the Corsair's lumped model, §9e)
> before the school flew anything. On today's law (§10), El Alamein's
> Spitfire does **not** hang on its propeller: straight up it sheds 13.5 m/s a
> second from 70 m/s and stops about 160 m higher. A 45 degree dive at full
> throttle reaches 105 m/s in ten seconds, so boom and zoom is alive. S does
> brake hard, but only after 3 to 4 seconds. The measured numbers, and the
> lessons built on them, are in [README.md](README.md), "What the airframe
> does". The list is kept as the record of what the docs said.

The repo has measured the retail flight model in detail
(`features/flyable-vehicles/flight-model.md`, the Corsair table, and the ledger
rows it cites). Several findings bend the real-world rules above, and the school
must decide, lesson by lesson, whether it teaches WWII doctrine or what works in
this game:

- **Thrust is strongest when the plane is slowest.** The engine's thrust law
  peaks at zero airspeed. A Corsair's thrust-to-weight is about 1.4 at rest,
  still above 1 at 20 m/s, and only about a third at cruise. So a fighter at
  full throttle can climb straight up, slow down, and then stop slowing: below
  roughly 25 m/s the propeller out-pulls gravity and the plane hangs or even
  climbs. **Hanging on the prop is a real BF1942 behaviour**.
  The real-world zoom climb, where the slower plane stalls out first, mostly does
  not happen. "Never follow a faster plane up" becomes much weaker: the pursuer
  can follow you up.
- **Diving gains far less speed than in reality.** Past about 70 m/s the
  propeller turns into an airbrake. A Corsair held vertical at idle peaks near
  92 m/s and settles near 54 m/s, against a level top speed of about 49 to 56
  m/s. A dive does not buy the big speed margin that boom and zoom relies on, so
  energy fights are compressed and turning fights dominate.
- **There is a hard lift ceiling at 1000 m.** Air density falls linearly to zero
  there, and the wings make nothing. Height advantage exists, but the bank is
  small; best service ceiling measured is about 989 m.
- **S is reverse thrust, not just idle.** The throttle can go negative (the
  Corsair to -0.6), and the propeller pushes backwards. This is what makes the
  forum's "turn in place" throttle trick work, and it makes the forced overshoot
  (throttle chop) the strongest defensive move in the game. It is the move the
  bots should use and the player most needs a counter to.
- **Loops are cheap.** A Corsair closes a loop from level flight in 7 to 9
  seconds, gains about 100 m, and is down to about 20 m/s over the top. It can
  string three in a row. The vertical fight is always available.
- **Roll is very fast.** Full-stick roll at cruise is about 212 degrees per
  second, quicker than any real WWII fighter. Reversals and scissors happen much
  faster than in real life, and the Zero's real high-speed roll weakness does not
  exist unless the data gives it one.
- **Stall is around 17 m/s.** Below that no angle of attack holds the plane up.
  A hidden lift regulator also holds BF1942 planes level "on rails", which is
  why they feel stable and why there is no flaps key.
- **No pilot blackout or g-damage** (per the Wake Island fan blog; the repo's
  notes do not record a g limit either). Players can pull as hard as they like,
  all the time; the only cost of a hard turn is speed.

**Implications for the bots and the lessons:**

1. The decisive BF1942 moves are probably the **throttle chop with reverse
   thrust**, the **loop and Immelmann** (cheap and always available), the
   **vertical hang**, and **flat and rolling scissors** with very fast rolls.
   Real-world boom and zoom still works for surprise, but the energy margin is
   small and the victim can follow you up.
2. Teach the real doctrine as the History Channel would tell it, then show what
   the game does with it ("in 1942 the slower plane stalled first; here, both
   can hang on the prop, so the one who points his nose first wins").
3. The counter to almost every BF1942 defensive trick is the same: **watch his
   speed, not just his position, and pull out of his plane instead of following
   into it.**
