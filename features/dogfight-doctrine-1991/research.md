# Jet-age air combat doctrine for a Desert Combat rules card

Research notes, 2026-11-10. Web research only. Nothing here was checked
against the game. Numbers in square brackets point to the source list at the
end.

Scope: within-visual-range jet combat (BFM and ACM), because Desert Combat's
jets fight with a cannon and short-range missiles under a low ceiling. Radar
missile doctrine is left out except where it explains why a real fight went
the way it did.

How to read the quotes:

- Every quote is under 15 words.
- Quotes from Boyd [3] were read directly from an OCR of the scan. Spelling
  is corrected where the OCR was plainly wrong.
- Quotes from Blesse [1] were read from page images of the foreword, the
  preface and the table of contents. The body of his manual was not read in
  the first pass; its two lists were read afterwards by OCR of the same scan
  and are in "Addendum: Blesse's two lists" at the end.
- Quotes and paragraph numbers from AFTTP 3-3 [6], and page numbers from Shaw
  [5], came through an automated reader over archive.org OCR text. Shaw's page
  numbers are from the running headers and may be one page off. Check wording
  against the page before printing a quote on a card.
- Shaw's book is in copyright, so his own prose is paraphrased here, not
  quoted. The quoted words on the card come from US government manuals.

## 1. The written rule sets

### 1.1 What exists

| Rule set | Who, when | What it is | Read for this page |

| *Aerial Attack Study* | Capt. John R. Boyd, USAF, 1960; Report 50-10-6C, revised 11 August 1964 | The first manual to work out each fighter manoeuvre and its counter from the weapon's envelope | Read through (OCR of the 156-page scan) [3] |
| Energy-manoeuvrability | Boyd and Thomas P. Christie, Eglin AFB, 1964; report APGC-TR-66-4, 1966 | One number for a fighter's ability to gain or lose energy: specific excess power | Secondary only [4]; Shaw's appendix [5] |

Notes on each.

**Blesse.** The foreword of the reprint read here is by Maj. Robert A.
Windoffer, Chief of the Tac/Eval Stand Section. It says Blesse studied and
flew air tactics at Nellis AFB "for almost two years", and that the manual
needed only minor editing to bring it up to date.
It calls the contents "fundamental principles" that "will remain a solid
foundation". Blesse signs the preface as Major, USAF, Commander, 3596th Combat
Crew Training Squadron, Nellis AFB. The preface's point is one word:
aggressiveness. "It is this pilot aggressiveness which we seek." [1]

The table of contents [1] gives the shape of a card on its own:

- Section I, The Offensive (p. 1): Objectives of Tactical Formation;
  Offensive Tactical Formation; High Altitude Formation Flying; The Element;
  Flight Leaders; Combat Tactics, Offensive Tactics; **Basic Principles of
  Offense** (p. 26).
- Section II, The Defensive (p. 33): Defensive Tactics; Calling Breaks;
  Reversing a Turn; Scissor Maneuver; Diving Spiral; Last Ditch Maneuver;
  Minimum Fuel Returns; Defensive Tactics (Specific); **Basic Principles of
  Defense** (p. 55).
- Section III, Related Subjects (p. 57): Employment of Fighters; Selection of
  Flight and Element Leaders in Combat; Voice Procedure; Speed Brake
  Utilization; Mach .9 Slat; Guns and Gunsights; Tracer Ammunition; Fifty
  Caliber versus Twenty MM; Binoculars in Combat; Conclusion (p. 74).

The two "Basic Principles" lists on pages 26 and 55 are the closest thing in
the jet age to Boelcke's eight. **They were not read.** They are the first
thing to fetch before a card is finalised.

Wikipedia dates the manual 1955, says it grew from a handout for visiting
squadrons, that the Air Force kept it classified for about two decades, and
that 3,000 copies were reprinted for tactical units as late as 1973 [2]. A
sale listing describes a copy as the *Fighter Weapons Newsletter* of March
1955 (search result only, **unverified**). Shaw prints one line of Blesse as
an epigraph to his chapter on basic fighter manoeuvres: "No guts, no glory",
and that to shoot a man down "you have to get in there and mix it up with
him" [5, p. 62].

**Boyd.** The study opens with the principle the whole card rests on: tactics
follow from the weapon. Effective tactics are "the best way to employ a given
weapon against a given enemy" [3, Introduction]. Part I is fighter against
bomber. Part II is fighter against fighter: the defensive turn, the scissors,
the high-speed yo-yo, the barrel-roll attack, the low-speed yo-yo, the
vertical rolling scissors, then tactical formation and flight tactics. Every
manoeuvre has a numbered "Procedures" list and a counter. The weapons are the
20 mm cannon and the rear-aspect AIM-9B, which had to be fired from a cone
behind the target under 2 g. That is very close to Desert Combat's weapons
(section 4).

**Energy-manoeuvrability.** Specific excess power is `Ps = V(T - D)/W`:
speed times thrust minus drag, over weight. It is the rate at which a fighter
can gain or lose energy. Wikipedia gives the two-volume Eglin report as 1964
and the numbered report APGC-TR-66-4 as 1966, and says the theory shaped the
requirements for the F-15 and F-16 [4]. Shaw's appendix defines specific
energy as height plus speed squared over twice g, and energy manoeuvrability
as the ability to change energy state [5, pp. 394-395].

**Topgun.** Capt. Frank Ault's 480-page *Air-to-Air Missile System Capability
Review* came out in early 1968 with nearly 250 recommendations. One was a
postgraduate fighter weapons school at Miramar. Lt. Cdr. Dan Pedersen built it
between December 1968 and March 1969. Before it, Navy fighters downed barely
two MiGs for each loss. By 1973 the Navy claimed about 12 to 1; later counts
put it nearer 9 to 1, against about 2 to 1 for the Air Force [7]. The school
flew its F-4 crews against A-4s and borrowed T-38s standing in for the MiG-17
and MiG-21 [8]. No Topgun document was read. What it taught is taken here
from Shaw, a Navy fighter pilot, whose chapter 5 sets out the Navy's "loose
deuce" [5, pp. 214-222].

**Shaw.** Chapter 2 names the manoeuvres (pursuit curves, lag displacement
rolls, high and low yo-yo, lead turn, nose-to-nose and nose-to-tail turns,
flat and rolling scissors, defensive spiral). Chapter 3 gives two ways to
fight a like aircraft: the **angles fight** (take position first) and the
**energy fight** (take an energy margin first, then cash it in) [5, p. 99].
Chapter 4 says which to choose against an unlike aircraft. Chapter 5 covers
the pair. Epigraphs from Boelcke, Mannock, Richthofen, Malan's generation,
Blesse, Olds and Cunningham run through the book, and the full Dicta Boelcke
is printed in chapter 7 [5, p. 274].

**AFTTP 3-3.** The F-16 manual states BFM's objectives as bullet lists, which
is how a card reads. Offensive BFM: kill; stay offensive; manoeuvre to the
weapons employment zone (WEZ); "Employ valid ordnance" [6, 4.3.9.1].
Defensive BFM: deny the WEZ and the ordnance; "Create maximum geometry
problems for attacker"; recognise and exploit errors; effective jinks and
infrared missile defence [6, 4.3.10.1]. High-aspect BFM (the head-on merge) is
4.3.11.

Supporting detail for the rows above.

**Rule 2, energy.** Shaw: potential energy (height) is limited, so energy lost
in turns ends as lost speed; an energy advantage can be extra speed, extra
height, or both [5, pp. 100, 131]. A vertical pull-up is the most efficient
way to turn spare speed into height [5, p. 118]. One of Shaw's epigraphs,
from Cdr William Driscoll, makes the same point about speed covering for
mistakes [5, p. 294].

**Rule 4, corner speed.** Shaw: corner speed is the lowest speed at which the
jet can pull its limit g, and it gives maximum instantaneous turn
performance. Turn radius goes with speed squared over g; turn rate goes with
g over speed [5, pp. 388-390]. Instantaneous turn is what the jet can do for
a moment. Sustained turn is what it can hold without losing speed, and it
goes with thrust-to-weight [5, pp. 388, 409]. The F-16 manual says its
fly-by-wire jet "does not have a true corner velocity", only a "corner
plateau" between 330 and 440 KCAS: radius is about constant from 170 to 330
KCAS, grows a little to 440, and grows fast above that [6, 4.3.4]. It also
tells a defender to hold about 275 KCAS by lowering the lift vector once
below 300 knots [6, 4.3.10.4.5.2].

**Rule 5, the circles.** Shaw: in a nose-to-nose turn, turn rate matters
little and the smaller radius wins. In a nose-to-tail turn, turn rate
produces the advantage [5, pp. 79-81]. The flat scissors is a string of
nose-to-nose turns and overshoots [5, p. 82]. AFTTP adds the lead turn at the
pass: a nose-low, lift-vector-on, maximum pull begun before the two aircraft
cross, to cut the angle between them [6, 4.3.11.1.9].

**Rule 6, pursuit and the control zone.** AFTTP: "There are three available
attack pursuit courses: lead, lag, and pure", set by where the attacker's
nose or lift vector points [6, 4.3.2.2]. The manual times the attacker's turn
to the moment of entering the bandit's turn circle [6, 4.3.9.5]. It uses the
terms "control zone (CZ)" and "elbow" for the stable place behind the bandit
[6, 4.3.9.5.3.3, 4.3.10.4.2.1], but the parts read give no dimensions. A
search result described the Navy T-45 syllabus's control zone as a cone 2,000
to 4,000 ft behind the bandit; the syllabus itself could not be opened
(**unverified**).

**Rule 7, the yo-yos.** Boyd defines the high-speed yo-yo as an offensive
tactic flown through both planes "to prevent an overshoot in the plane of the
defender's turn" [3, printed p. 64]. Timing is the skill: too early and the
defender dives away; too late and the defender reverses up into you. His
low-speed yo-yo is for an attacker at six o'clock with no closure: afterburner
on, dive below and inside, then pull up into a firing position [3]. Shaw's
versions are the same in purpose: the high yo-yo stops an overshoot and cuts
angle off; the low yo-yo adds closure and angles with a lead-pursuit move out
of plane [5, pp. 72-73].

**Rule 9, defence.** Boyd's defensive turn has five procedures. The key ones:
judge range and angle; make a hard turn, not a maximum one, because a maximum
turn "will kill off airspeed very rapidly"; tighten to maximum only as the
attacker nears gun range [3, printed p. 53]. If the attacker overshoots, the
scissors, "a series of turn reversals", takes the offensive back [3]. AFTTP
on the gun: an early jink solves the bandit's problem for them; "the lethal
gunshot is coming first, and jink timing is frequently late"; the preferred
jink is a rolling duck-under, which needs 1,000 to 1,500 ft of height; idle
power and speed brakes give the attacker the worst closure problem [6,
4.3.10.4.1, 4.3.10.5.2, 4.3.10.5.3, 4.3.10.5.5]. Shaw: a break turn is
wasteful when a hard turn will do [5, p. 132].

**Rule 10, the pair.** Three doctrines, in Shaw's order [5, ch. 5].
*Fighting wing* ("welded wing"): a leader who fights and a wingman who hangs
on and looks behind. *Double attack*: the pair splits and attacks in turn.
*Loose deuce*: an engaged fighter keeps pressure on the bandit to make it
predictable, a free fighter kills it as fast as it safely can, and the roles
swap on a call. Shaw's criticism of fighting wing is that the wingman, busy
holding position, sees little [5, p. 199]. Boyd's 1964 text is the Air Force
side: the fluid four gives look-out and mutual support going in, then
"will become nothing more than individual fighting elements" once the fight
starts [3, printed p. 117]. AFTTP in 1999 has the Navy's two roles under Air
Force names, engaged and supporting, and gives the supporting fighter two
first duties: keep clear of the engaged fighter's flight path, and do not
become defensive [6, 4.4.5.3]. "Formation integrity is an integral part of
all element maneuvering." [6, 4.4.2]

**Rule 11, the plan.** Shaw's summary of the one-against-one chapter is that
victory comes from a plan made before the fight and flown aggressively [5,
p. 138]. Against a jet that turns better, the high thrust-to-weight jet
should fight for energy; the jet with the low wing loading should fight for
angles [5, p. 142]. A jet worse in both should not stay [5, p. 183].

**Rule 12, leaving.** Shaw on a single fighter among many: keep turns level
or nose-low to hold speed, turn no more than about 90 degrees one way without
a reversal or a look behind, and call the bugout on the radio [5, pp.
282-283].

## 2. Fights that could be staged

### 2.1 The Gulf War, 1991

The USAF counts 39 air-to-air victories in the war, 36 of them by F-15s
[32]. Almost all were AIM-7 radar missile shots set up by AWACS [10], [14].
Wikipedia credits the AIM-9M with all ten Sidewinder kills [30], though one
list gives al-Shamrani's missiles as AIM-9P [13]. Two kills were by
gun, both A-10s on helicopters, and two were "manoeuvring" kills where an
Iraqi aircraft flew into the ground [14]. So the war has few visual, turning
fights. The candidates, best first.

#### A. Rodriguez and the MiG-29, 19 January 1991 (the best fit)

One list calls it the war's only turning fight [13].

| | |

| Second MiG | About 10 miles behind, to the north. Underhill's system showed it as friendly, so no shot. Rodriguez closed to identify it by eye [10], [12] |
| The turning fight | The two merged and both turned left. A descending spiral from about 8,000 ft at about 600 mph. Rodriguez tried an AIM-9, but the hot ground spoiled the lock; he changed to AIM-7. He closed to about 1,500 ft, inside gun range, without a firing position [10], [12] |
| The end | Near the ground, the MiG rolled inverted and pulled through in a split-S with afterburner lit, and flew into the desert. No weapon was fired. No ejection was seen [12] |
| Heights disagree | The fight was "under 1,000 ft" above the terrain when the MiG rolled [12]; the split-S began at about 2,000 ft [13]; or at about 600 ft [11] |

Why it teaches: it is rule 3 (he had to see it to know what it was), rule 6
(inside 1,500 ft and still no shot is what losing the control zone looks
like), rule 9 from the loser's side (a defensive move that needs height the
defender does not have), and it happens under a low floor with short-range
weapons that will not fire. It needs no radar missile to stage the second
half. The game has the same floor.

Why it teaches: the heat seeker fails against a low helicopter over hot
ground, and the answer is the slashing gun pass that the J-CATCH trials
arrived at twelve years earlier (section 3). Both aircraft types have
counterparts in the mod.

#### C. Al-Shamrani's double, 24 January 1991 (best stern Sidewinder attack)

Capt. Ayedh al-Shamrani, No. 13 Squadron, Royal Saudi Air Force, F-15C
80-0068 [13]. Iraqi Mirage F1s from al-Kut set out to strike Saudi oil
facilities and came down the Gulf behind a US Navy strike's return route.
AWACS sent two Saudi pairs. Al-Shamrani got behind both Mirages and shot both
down with Sidewinders, AIM-9P by one list, then landed at Dhahran short of
fuel [10], [13]. It is rule 6 and rule 8 in the plainest form: a stern
conversion and two rear-quarter heat-seeker shots, and rule 12 at the end.
Range and height at the shots were not found.

#### D. Fox and Mongillo, 17 January 1991 (a head-on pass, not a dogfight)

Lt. Cdr. Mark Fox (F/A-18C 163508) and Lt. Nick Mongillo (163502), VFA-81,
USS *Saratoga*, in a four-ship carrying four 2,000 lb Mk 84 bombs each to
H-3 airfield (Al Walid) [15], [16]. An E-2C called bandits on the nose at 15
miles. Two Iraqi MiG-21s (F-7s by one list) came head-on at about Mach 1.2
to 1.3; the Hornets were at about 30,000 ft and Mach 0.9; closure was over
1,200 knots [15], [16]. Fox locked at about 9 miles, fired an AIM-9, saw no
smoke, and fired an AIM-7 at about 4 miles. The AIM-9 hit first [13], [16].
Mongillo's AIM-7 took the second. Fox said the MiGs did not manoeuvre. From
the call to the hits was under 40 seconds, and all four Hornets kept their
bombs and went on to bomb the airfield [15]. It teaches rule 8 and the
all-aspect missile (section 4). It is not a turning fight.

#### E. Speicher, and the MiG-25s

- **17 January 1991.** Lt. Cdr. Scott Speicher, VFA-81, F/A-18C 163484, was
  shot down about 100 miles west of Baghdad at 28,000 ft and Mach 0.92. The
  most likely cause is an R-40 missile from a MiG-25PD of the IQAF's 96th
  Squadron flown by Zuhair Dawoud, fired from about 29 km. The Navy's 1997
  history said a surface-to-air missile; a 2001 CIA summary said an aircraft
  [10], [17]. A radar missile at night: not a visual fight.
- **19 January 1991.** Capt. Rick Tollini and Capt. Larry Pitts, 58th TFS,
  each shot down a MiG-25 with an AIM-7M. Pitts followed his target down in a
  split-S to low level and fired several missiles before one hit [10], [13].
  The missile-by-missile sequence was not found in a source read here.
- **30 January 1991, the "Samurra air battle".** Two MiG-25PDS were vectored
  onto a pair of F-15Cs of the 53rd TFS while the other pair was at the
  tanker. Both sides fired several missiles; none is confirmed to have
  hit. Iraq claimed one F-15, later two; the USAF records no loss or
  damage. The Wikipedia article carries a notice that its stated result is
  disputed [18]. It was fought beyond visual range at high speed and does not
  fit the mod.

#### F. The F-15E and the Hind, 14 February 1991

Capt. Richard "TB" Bennett and Capt. Dan "Chewie" Bakke, F-15E 89-0487,
335th TFS, callsign PACKARD 41, on a Scud patrol. AWACS sent them against
helicopters landing troops near a US special forces team. Bakke found them in
the targeting pod and released a 2,000 lb GBU-10 laser-guided bomb at one on
the ground. It lifted off; he held the laser on it and the bomb hit it in the
air [19]. One list dates this 4 February [14]; two give 14 February [13],
[19]. The type is an Mi-24 in [10] and [19], a Hughes 500 in [13]. Shaw had
written six years earlier that bombs may be the best low-level weapon against
a helicopter [5, p. 192].

#### G. Iraqi claims

- A MiG-23 hit an F-111 with an R-24T on the first night; the F-111 flew
  home [10].
- A MiG-29 flown by Jameel Sayhood is claimed to have shot down an RAF
  Tornado with an R-60 on 19 January. British records put that Tornado's loss
  on 22 January on another mission [10].
- An EF-111 crew was credited by some with making a Mirage F1 fly into the
  ground on the first night; an F-15 pilot in the area did not recall an
  EF-111 there [10].
- A MiG-29 hit on a B-52 is often repeated. Nothing read here supports it
  (**unverified**).

None of these is documented well enough to stage.

### 2.2 Earlier fights that wrote the doctrine

**Cunningham and Driscoll against a MiG-17, 10 May 1972.** Lt. Randy
Cunningham and Lt. (jg) William Driscoll, F-4J, VF-96, USS *Constellation*
[33]. Cunningham's own account is printed in Shaw [5, pp. 176-177]. In order:
a head-on pass in which the MiG fired its cannon; both aircraft pulling
straight up, canopy to canopy a few hundred feet apart; a rolling scissors in
the vertical, which the slower, tighter MiG was winning; on the last
pull-up, Cunningham went to idle and put out the speed brakes so the MiG shot
out in front; the MiG pitched over and dived away; a Sidewinder shot down the
dive. On the way home the F-4 was hit by a surface-to-air missile and the
crew ejected over water [33]. The pilot was said to be an ace called "Colonel
Tomb" or "Toon". No such officer existed [21], [22].

Why it teaches: it is the textbook case for rules 5, 7 and 11. A heavy,
high-thrust jet took an angles fighter into the vertical, was nearly beaten
at it, and won with one deliberate overshoot forced on the other side. It is
a gun-range fight ended by a rear-quarter heat seeker, which is the mod's
weapon set. If one historical fight is staged as the lesson for "the
vertical", this is it.

**Operation Bolo, 2 January 1967.** Col. Robin Olds's 8th Tactical Fighter
Wing flew F-4Cs along the F-105 bombers' routes, heights, speeds and radio
calls, with the bombers' jamming pods, to draw the MiG-21s up. Seven MiG-21s
were claimed for no loss in about 12 minutes; Vietnamese records admit five.
Olds's own kill was a roll up and over a tighter-turning MiG to drop in
behind it for two AIM-9s. The MiG-21s were stood down for months [23]. It
teaches rule 1 in Boelcke's sense (the advantage is arranged before the
fight) and rule 7.

**The Bekaa Valley, 9 to 10 June 1982.** Israeli F-15s and F-16s, directed by
E-2Cs with Syrian ground control jammed, shot down about 82 Syrian aircraft
for no loss in air combat [24]. It is a lesson about seeing first, at the
scale of an air force. The individual fights are not documented in what was
read.

**The Falklands, May to June 1982.** Sea Harriers shot down 20 Argentine
aircraft in air combat for no loss in air combat, armed with the all-aspect
AIM-9L [30]. The Mirages and Daggers were faster but at the end of their range,
with about five minutes over the islands. Thrust vectoring in forward flight
was not used in combat; a pilot is quoted calling it a last-ditch move [25].
It matters for the mod's Harrier: the real aircraft won with a missile and
with the other side's fuel state, not by stopping in mid-air. Shaw says the
same of V/STOL: the hover is "often highly overrated" in air combat, and
vectoring belongs to the last-ditch defence or the final shot [5, p. 187].

## 3. Helicopters in the same sky

### 3.1 J-CATCH, 1978 to 1979

The Joint Countering Attack Helicopter trials, US Army and US Air Force
[26], [27].

- Phase I, May 1978: simulator work at NASA Langley. Phase II: helicopter
  against helicopter at Fort Rucker, AH-1s and OH-58s against UH-1Ns and
  CH-3Es playing the Soviet side. Phase III: helicopters against F-4, A-7,
  A-10 and F-15 over two weeks. Later phases in 1979 turned the results into
  joint tactics [26].
- Wikipedia's headline: the helicopters won close-range gun fights about 5 to
  1, and most fighter pilots did not know they had been shot until the
  debrief [26].
- A 1986 Army monograph, working from the trial's final report, gives the
  fixed-wing figures by weapon, fighter kills to helicopter kills: F-15 with
  AIM-7F and AIM-9L 2.9 to 1; A-10 with the 30 mm gun 1.3 to 1; F-4 with the
  20 mm gun 0.7 to 1. The same monograph also cites a best ratio of 1.7 to 1
  without reconciling the two [27, pp. 20-22].
- Air Force crews found the helicopter "extremely difficult to hit" and
  "virtually impossible to track". The technique that worked was "a slashing
  attack, a quick surprise attack". Reports called the helicopter "as
  dangerous as a ground gun emplacement" [27, p. 19].
- The F-15's radar found helicopters beyond 64 km, but the crews saw them
  only at 6 to 9 km. The AIM-9L from about 2,500 m was the best weapon. Guns
  worked to about 1,400 m (20 mm) and 1,550 m (30 mm). The AIM-7 was not
  recommended against low targets because of ground clutter [27, pp. 20-21].
- The helicopters won by terrain masking, by working in teams, and by taking
  shots the fighter did not see. They carried no air-to-air missiles in the
  trial [27, p. 21].
- Conclusion for the jet: stay out of a turning fight with a helicopter.
  Shoot from range and from above, with a look-down missile, or make one fast
  pass and leave [26], [27].

### 3.2 The Army's own guidance

- **FM 1-107, *Air-to-Air Combat*, Department of the Army, 1984.** The manual
  exists and is cited in [27]'s bibliography. It began as FM 1-105 in April
  1983. A workshop at Fort Rucker from 30 January to 3 February 1984 produced
  its final draft [29]. By December 1986 it was "being revised" [27, p. 24].
  **The manual itself was not found online and was not read.**
- What the 1984 workshop report says of its contents: chapter 5 covers
  helicopter against helicopter and helicopter against fixed-wing; chapter 6
  covers air combat manoeuvring, which "may be used when operation in the
  terrain flight environment is no longer feasible". Its recommendations: all
  aviation units to train for air-to-air combat; an air-to-air missile for
  long range and a fast-firing cannon for short range; rear-view mirrors [29].
- The companion article in the same issue argues that the Army must win "low
  altitude air superiority" itself, and gives the helicopter's attack as "a
  fast pass from above, close-in shooting", with surprise, then leaving the
  area at once [29].
- Doctrine reversed in those years. The older attack-battalion manual, FM
  17-30, said dogfights would be avoided and enemy helicopters drawn onto air
  defences. FM 1-111 and FM 1-112 made counter-air a task of the aviation
  brigade. The 1986 monograph's own view: "speed and surprise are the two most
  important principles" of a helicopter air fight, most of which will happen
  inside 1,000 m, where missiles have little effect and the cannon decides
  [27, pp. 24-29].
- A 1979 Marine study of transport helicopters against the Mi-24 gives a
  fifteen-point checklist. The points that carry into a game: "Use terrain
  masking."; fly in pairs at least; know the fixed-wing evasive tactics. Its
  advice against an attacking jet is to "fly directly toward the attacking
  fighter", which forces the jet to steepen its dive and shortens its firing
  time [28, p. 25].

### 3.3 Shaw on jet against helicopter (1985)

Paraphrased from [5, pp. 188-194]:

- A helicopter cannot run from a jet. Its defence is to get as low as it can,
  speed up, and turn towards the attacker.
- The jet's attack is hardest when the helicopter does not fly straight at
  it or away from it.
- Turning with a helicopter gains nothing and is dangerous: the helicopter
  out-turns any jet at low speed and can point its gun.
- A heat seeker has trouble against a helicopter low over warm ground. A gun
  pass from above works. So, Shaw admits, do bombs.

### 3.4 A helicopter card, if one is wanted

Four lines cover what the sources agree on:

1. Stay low and use the ground (terrain masking) [27], [28].
2. When a jet dives on you, turn towards it [28], [5].
3. Never fly alone; the second helicopter takes the shot the jet does not see
   [27], [28].
4. Flying the jet: one fast pass from above, or a missile from range. Do not
   turn with it [26], [27], [5].

## 4. Where the real doctrine leans on things a simple game may not have

Listed so the school can say "the game differs" honestly. What Desert Combat
actually models is in the repo, not here; the one fact used below is from
`features/dogfight-doctrine/README.md`, "Research notes": the mod's AIM-9
flies straight and bursts within 7 m of an aircraft.

| The real thing | What doctrine does with it | If the game lacks it |
|---|---|---|
| **Radar missiles (AIM-7) and AWACS** | Most 1991 kills were AIM-7 shots at 4 to 16 miles on targets AWACS had called [10], [14]. Seeing first was done by radar | Every 1991 fight but Rodriguez's second MiG and the A-10 kills loses its first half. Rule 3 becomes purely about eyes |
| **All-aspect heat seekers (AIM-9L from 1977, first used in 1981 and 1982; AIM-9M in 1991)** | A shot is possible head-on, so "from behind" stopped being a rule for missiles. Fox's Sidewinder was fired head-on [16], [30] | With a missile that flies straight, the mod is back before the AIM-9B. Boelcke's rule 5 and Boyd's firing cone hold in full. Boyd's 1964 study, written for a gun and a rear-aspect missile, is the closest real manual to the mod's weapons |
| **A guided rear-aspect missile (AIM-9B)** | Boyd's defence starts with denying the missile: turn hard enough that the attacker exceeds 2 g before reaching the firing cone, then deal with the gun [3, printed pp. 51-53] | With no guidance there is no launch limit to exploit and no need for flares or a break towards the sun. Missile defence collapses into guns defence: be out of the line of the shot |
| **G limits and blackout** | The F-15 is cleared to 9 g [32]. An untrained person can black out at 4 to 6 g; a g-suit and straining raise that [31]. Corner speed exists because of the structural g limit [5, p. 389] | If the game has no g limit and no blackout, there is no corner speed in the real sense: the best turn is wherever the flight model's lift and drag put it. Rule 4 must be measured, not assumed |
| **Energy bleed in a hard turn** | The whole of rule 2. Boyd's defender does not turn at maximum because it "will kill off airspeed very rapidly" [3] | If the jets hold speed in a maximum turn, a hard turn costs nothing, and the yo-yos of rule 7 lose their reason. The existing README already notes the jets "do not run out of speed going up" |
| **Thrust-to-weight over 1** | The F-15 can accelerate going straight up at combat weight [32]. That is what makes the energy fight and the vertical work for it and not for an A-10 or Su-25 | If every jet climbs alike, rule 11 has nothing to choose between. If none runs out of speed going up, the vertical is free for all |
| **Afterburner fuel** | Fuel is the clock on every fight. Boyd lists fuel management with look-out and manoeuvrability [3, printed p. 117]; al-Shamrani landed short of fuel [10]; the Argentine jets had five minutes over the Falklands [25] | With unlimited fuel, rule 12 has only ammunition and damage to rest on |
| **A real ceiling** | Fights in 1972 and 1991 began at 8,000 to 30,000 ft and came down. Height is energy in the bank | Under a ceiling of about 1,000 m there is little height to trade. Rodriguez's fight, which ended below 1,000 ft, is the real case nearest to it |
| **Identification** | Rodriguez had to close to see what the second aircraft was; the first three flights of Operation Bolo were cleared to fire without seeing [10], [23] | A game marks friend and foe. The visual merge that real rules forced does not have to happen |
| **Radar warning and a second pair of eyes** | Boyd says the four exists for look-out, and that with tail-warning radar the pair or the single ship would do [3, printed p. 117] | A game's map, markers and third-person view do the wingman's look-out job. Rule 10's first reason weakens; its second (the free fighter's shot) remains |
| **Heat seekers against a low helicopter** | The AIM-9 would not lock for Swain or Sheehy; J-CATCH found the same [20], [27] | A straight-flying missile with a proximity fuse does not care about ground heat. The gun-pass lesson still holds because turning with a helicopter still loses |
| **Thrust vectoring (Harrier)** | Not used in combat in 1982 [25]; Shaw rates it a last-ditch move [5, p. 187] | If the mod's Harrier can stop and pivot cheaply, it will be stronger in a turning fight than the real one ever was |

## Addendum: Blesse's two lists (2026-11-10)

The first pass could not reach the body of *No Guts, No Glory* [1]. The same
76-page scan was fetched whole and read by OCR afterwards (macOS Vision, the
scan's pages 30 to 34 and 58; printed pages 26 to 28 and 55). OCR slips are
put right where the word is plain. It is a US Air Force publication of 1955.

**Basic principles of offense** (24, printed pp. 26-28), the ones a card
could use, by Blesse's own number:

- 1: the element of two is the "most effective basic fighting team"; 2: two
  elements make the flight.
- 4: "When in doubt in a dogfight, trade airspeed for altitude."
- 6: stop turning with an aircraft you can no longer track with the sight:
  "Pull up immediately and keep your nose behind his tail."
- 7: if speed brakes will drift you into the target's turn radius, use them
  "in preference to the YO YO maneuver".
- 8: "Cruise at a high Mach."
- 9: "Look around; you can't shoot anything until you see it."
- 10: "Keep the aircraft you are attacking in sight."
- 12: "Attack from low and behind whenever possible."
- 13: with an enemy in front, "assume there is one behind".
- 14: "Know the performance data on all aircraft you are apt to be fighting."
- 16: assume every pilot you meet is the world's best until shown otherwise.
- 17: do not shoot unless positive it is an enemy: "If you can't tell, you
  are out of range."
- 18: destroying an aircraft is three phases: manoeuvring 85%, positioning
  the pipper 10%, firing and adjusting the burst 5%.
- 21: "Play on the team - no individualists."
- 22: "When in doubt - attack!"
- 24: split the enemy's formation, and their mutual support fails.

**Basic principles of defense** (19, printed p. 55):

- 1: if you slow down, have an element high and fast for support.
- 2: "Except at extreme ranges, always turn into the attack."
- 4: "Keep your attacker at a high angle off."
- 5: keep airspeeds up when patrolling.
- 6: never reverse a turn unless the attacker is sliding to the outside of it.
- 8: lose your wingman, and both of you leave the combat area.
- 10: "Have a 'last ditch' maneuver and practice it."
- 11: "Keep a close check on your fuel."
- 13: when told to break, do it.
- 15: "Watch the sun": a well-planned attack comes out of it.
- 16: mutual support is to sandwich the attacker between the defenders.
- 17: the defender's object is lateral separation; then a reverse and a
  series of scissors puts the attacker in front.

What this changes above:

- Rule 1 of the proposed card can quote Blesse directly (offense 22), and
  rule 11 has a primary source (offense 14) where it had only Shaw.
- Rules 3, 6, 9, 10 and 12 each have a 1955 line behind them (offense 10 and
  12, defense 2, offense 1, defense 11).
- Offense 4 runs the other way from the Boyd line quoted for rule 2: in
  doubt, Blesse buys height with speed; Boyd's low-speed yo-yo buys speed
  with height. Both are the one account.
- Defense 15 keeps Boelcke's sun in 1955.
- "Not found or not verified" no longer applies to Blesse's two lists; the
  rest of the body (the manoeuvre sections) is still unread.
