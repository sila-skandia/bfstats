# Tank doctrine, 1991: research for a "rules of 1991" card (Desert Combat)

Status: research only (2026-11-10). Nothing in the repo was changed.

The brief: do for Desert Combat what `features/tank-doctrine-1918` did for
BF1918. Find the written doctrine of the 1991 Gulf War that a card of ten to
twelve rules could be built from. Find the real fights that could be staged.
Collect the hard numbers a game's data can be checked against. Say where the
real doctrine leans on something a simple game may not have.

Numbers in square brackets are the sources at the end. Every quote is the
source's own words and is under 15 words.

## How this was read, and its limits

- Most of the manuals were read as the full text that archive.org makes from
  its scans. Scan errors are common. Where a quoted word was plainly a scan
  error it was put right (for example "lime" for "time"). Page numbers are
  given only where the page footer could be read next to the passage.
- **FM 17-12-1, "Tank Combat Tables M1" / "Tank Gunnery (Abrams)", was not
  found online in any edition.** Two Army Research Institute reports written
  from it were read instead. One is a 1989 analysis that says it is built on
  "FM 17-12-1, with change (w/c) 2, 1988" [3]. The other is a 1984 set of
  crew booklets written with the Armor School's Weapons Department [4]. Every
  gunnery rule below is cited to those two, not to the manual itself.
- **FM 17-15, "Tank Platoon": the 1987 edition in force in 1991 was not
  found.** The text read is the later edition that covers the M1A2 (dated
  1 April 1996 on EverySpec [35]; the archive.org copy is labelled 1991) [2].
  The wingman, the three movement techniques and the three fire patterns are
  quoted from it. FM 71-1 of 1988 [1], which was in force, gives the same
  movement techniques at company level.
- **FM 100-5: the 1982 edition was read** [6]. The 1986 edition was not opened.
- FM 23-34 (TOW) was read in its 1994 edition [10]. The Worldwide Equipment
  Guide is the 1999 edition [19].
- globalsecurity.org, DTIC and the Wayback Machine refused automated reads.
  The Fort Benning copy of McMaster's 1991 typescript did not resolve. His
  2016 retelling was read instead [17].
- Wikipedia pages are used for battle totals and a few vehicle figures. They
  are marked "secondary" each time.

## Part 1. The card: twelve rules of 1991

Above the card sits AirLand Battle. FM 100-5 (1982) names its tenets:
"initiative, depth, agility, and synchronization" [6, ch. 2]. It asks for
"Speed and violence" [6, p. 7-2]. It says "At night the advantage also belongs
to the attacker." [6, p. 3-1]. The twelve rules below are what that meant for
one tank crew, one platoon and the people shooting at them.

The WWI rule numbers are those of `features/tank-doctrine-1918/README.md`.

### Rule 1. See first, shoot first

**Rule.** Search with the thermal sight all the time, by day as well as by
night. It sees through dust, smoke and dark. The crew that sees first fires
first, and the first shot usually ends it.

**Sources.**
- FM 71-1, *Tank and Mechanized Infantry Company Team*, 22 November 1988,
  ch. 1, p. 1-5 [1]: "Using thermal sights during the day as well as night
  decreases the obscuration".
- ARI Research Report 1532 (1989), describing FM 17-12-1 w/c 2 (1988),
  pp. 68-69 [3]: "the thermal channel is the preferred mode of operation".
- DoD, *Conduct of the Persian Gulf War*, Appendix T, April 1992, p. T-144
  [13]: M1A1 crews could "see first, shoot first". Of thermal sights on the
  other side: "Iraqi systems lacked this capability."
- *Certain Victory* (US Army, 1993), p. 1 [14]: "victory goes to the gunner
  who sees the other guy first".
- McMaster's second lesson from 73 Easting is to shoot first [17].

**Lineage.** New. A 1918 crew looked through slits and fought by day. SS 203's
massed rifle fire was meant to blind them further (WWI rule 8). In 1918 and in
1943 dust and dark hid both sides alike. In 1991 the storm hid one side only.

**The rule turns on:** how far each side can see, in clear air and in dust or
dark. At 73 Easting the eye saw 400 to 600 m and the thermal sight 1,500 m or
more [16, p. 13].

### Rule 2. Kill the most dangerous first

**Rule.** Of all the targets in view, shoot first the one that can kill you
and is about to. Among equals, take the nearer.

**Sources.**
- ARI Research Product 84-11, *Fire Commands for the M1 Tank*, May 1984, p. 14
  [4]. A most dangerous threat is one which "CAN KILL YOU, HAS SEEN YOU, and IS
  PREPARING TO ENGAGE YOU". (Three bullet points in the booklet, run together
  here.)
- ARI 1532, p. 35 [3]: targets are "engaged in order of classification with
  the most dangerous target being engaged first". Same class, different
  ranges: the nearer first (same page, citing FM 17-12-1 w/c 2).
- FM 71-1 (1988), p. 2-35 [1]: "Destroying the most dangerous target first,
  when in doubt."
- FM 17-15, ch. 2, "Distribution" [2]: a most dangerous threat is "any enemy
  antitank system preparing to engage the platoon".
- FM 1-112 (1991), ch. 3, "Attack Priorities" [11], says the same to the
  helicopter crew: "engage the nearest target that first poses a threat".

**Lineage.** New on paper. The 1917-18 instructions rank no targets.

**The rule turns on:** which enemy weapons can hurt your hull at this range,
and which way their guns point.

### Rule 3. Sabot for tanks, HEAT for the rest

**Rule.** The dart (sabot) is for tanks. HEAT is for light armour, bunkers and
trucks. Ride into contact with the round for the enemy you expect. If the
wrong round is in the gun, fire it at once and load the right one next.

**Sources.**
- ARI 1532, p. 38 [3]: "tanks with SABOT, PCs with HEAT, troops with coax".
- ARI 1532, p. 54 [3]: on a change of round, "The round in the tube is fired
  first".
- ARI 84-11, pp. 68-69 [4], gives the fire command for it: the commander
  names the loaded round, gives "FIRE", then "FIRE SABOT".
- *Certain Victory*, pp. 2-4 [14]. Eagle 66 met T-72s with HEAT in the gun.
  McMaster's order was "Fire, fire sabot". The HEAT round went at 1,420 m and
  cleared the berm by inches. The next two rounds were sabot. The book's
  comment: "With sabot, berms were no problem."

**Lineage.** Continues WWI rule 8 (do not waste the wrong weapon on a tank)
and the WWII split between armour-piercing shot and high explosive.

**The rule turns on:** the damage of each round against each kind of hull, and
whether a round is stopped by earth.

### Rule 4. Lase if you can; battlesight if you cannot

**Rule.** With time and a working laser, take the range and let the computer
lay the gun ("precision gunnery"). With no time, fire on the range already set
in the computer ("battlesight"). The dart flies so flat that one setting
covers every likely range.

**Sources.**
- ARI 1532, p. 53 [3]: "the most common battlesight ammunition and range
  setting is SABOT at 1200 meters". The same page gives the arithmetic. The
  round rises 0.8 m above the line of sight at about 605 m. Against 1.5 m of
  exposed tank the setting is good to 1,600 m.
- ARI 84-11, section G, "Direct Fire Techniques" [4]: battlesight is the
  commonest way to shoot when the fire control is not whole.
- FM 17-15, ch. 4 [2]: planning range is 2,000 m for the 105 mm gun and 2,500 m
  for the 120 mm.
- ARI 1532, p. 54 [3]: fire "can be effective out to and beyond 3000 meters".
- The other side had the same idea and it failed them. *Certain Victory*,
  p. 261 [14]: Iraqi guns were zeroed at "the standard Soviet battle sight
  range of 1,800 meters". The cavalry opened at 2,400 m and the Iraqi shots
  fell short.

**Lineage.** New in this form. A 6-pounder of 1918 and a 75 mm of 1943 both
needed the range judged for every shot.

**The rule turns on:** muzzle velocity and drop. It exists only where a round
stays within a tank's height of the line of sight for over a kilometre.

### Rule 5. Shoot on the move

**Rule.** Do not stop to fire. The gun is stabilised. A moving tank is hard to
hit, and an enemy who aims at your muzzle flash hits where you were.

**Sources.**
- ARI 1532, p. 57 [3]: a moving tank that works "will normally continue to
  move during an engagement". Page 58: if the stabiliser fails it "will
  normally fire the main gun from the short halt".
- DoD report, Appendix T, p. T-146 [13]: "Many first round hits occurred while
  the vehicle was on the move." Page T-144: many engagements were on the move
  at 15 to 25 km/h.
- *Certain Victory*, p. 261 [14]: Iraqi return fire "merely skipped over the
  spot where the Abrams had been".
- IDA D-1110, p. I-141 [15], Col. Bloedorn on Eagle Troop: "Straight on
  through, firing on the move."
- The T-72 could fire moving in theory. The Worldwide Equipment Guide, p. 4-17
  [19], allows it up to 25 km/h but adds "most crews may halt before firing".

**Lineage.** This is the rule that turned twice.
- 1918: SS 203 said fire on the move. Mitchell stopped to hit the A7V. The
  manual was wrong (WWI rule 2).
- 1943: FM 17-12, *Tank Gunnery*, 22 April 1943, para 47c [5]: beyond 600
  yards it is "always necessary to stop before firing". Para 55a: fire from a
  moving tank "is inaccurate and causes an uneconomical expenditure of
  ammunition". Para 55c: "halt the vehicle, destroy the target, then continue
  the advance".
- 1991: the manual says move again, and this time the gun can do it.

**The rule turns on:** whether the hull's speed and pitch reach the shell.

### Rule 6. Turret-down to look, hull-down to shoot; a berm is not armour

**Rule.** Wait where only the sights and the roof gun show. Roll forward until
the gun clears the ground, fire, and roll back (the crews' "berm drill"). Use
a fold in the ground or a dug hole. Earth piled up in front marks the tank and
stops no dart.

**Sources.**
- FM 17-15, ch. 4 [2]: turret-down positions "allow the tanks to fire only
  their caliber .50 or loader's M240 machine gun". The platoon then moves to
  hull-down positions to fire the main gun. The same chapter lists "berm
  drills" among the drills crews rehearse. It does not define the term.
- ARI 1532, p. 58 [3]: a tank that is turret-down "will move to a hull down
  position before firing".
- FM 17-15, figure 4-5 [2]: "Berms attract attention. Dig down, not up."
- FM 71-1 (1988), ch. 3 [1], quotes Patton's Letter of Instruction No. 3 of
  20 May 1944: the only cover with "any security is that afforded by earth
  defilade".
- TRADOC Bulletin 3 (1976), p. 15 [9]: a fully exposed tank is hit at "4 TIMES
  THAT OF HITTING A TARGET WHICH STOPS IN HULL DEFILADE".
- IDA D-1110, p. I-54 [15], Maj. Gen. Funk of 3rd Armored Division on Iraqi
  sand berms: "they won't stop a sabot round".

**Lineage.** Continues WWI rule 4 (off the skyline). FM 71-1 makes the link
itself by quoting a 1944 letter in a 1988 manual.

**The rule turns on:** how much of the hull stands above the gun, and whether
earth stops a shell.

### Rule 7. Never alone: wingman, overwatch, shared targets

**Rule.** A tank fights as one of a pair. When contact is expected, one group
moves while another sits still and covers it. The platoon splits the targets
so that no enemy is shot twice while another is left alone.

**Sources.**
- FM 17-15, ch. 1 [2]: "wingmen move, stop, and shoot when their leaders do".
- FM 17-15, ch. 3 [2], on bounding overwatch: "It is the most secure, but
  slowest, movement technique." Travelling is for when contact is unlikely.
  Travelling overwatch is for when it is possible.
- FM 71-1 (1988), p. 3-23 [1]: once contact is made, move by bounds, but
  "Tanks should not remain stationary any longer than necessary". Page 3-39:
  "Bounding overwatch is difficult at night".
- FM 17-15, ch. 2 [2], names three fire patterns: frontal, cross and depth.
  The frontal rule is "near to far, flank to center". The cross rule is
  "outside in, near to far".
- FM 71-1, p. 4-10 [1], on why fire is massed: "You cannot pick vehicles off
  one at a time".
- McMaster's ninth lesson is mutual support between platoons [17].

**Lineage.** Continues WWI rule 5 (never alone; SS 204's section of three). The
1991 book adds who moves and who watches. WWI rule 6 (spread out, 100 yards)
also continues: FM 71-1, p. 6-25, wants TOW squads 300 m apart [1].

**The rule turns on:** more than two tanks. Reload time against the number of
guns firing gives the arithmetic.

### Rule 8. Front to the enemy; shoot for his flank

**Rule.** Keep your thickest armour towards the gun that can hurt you. Place
yourself to hit him in the side or rear.

**Sources.**
- FM 71-1 (1988), p. 1-5 [1]: "Armored vehicles are less protected on the
  flanks and rear". Page 4-19: "Flank shots increase kill probabilities
  against enemy armored vehicles".
- FM 17-15, ch. 2 [2]: the cross pattern is used when frontal armour "requires
  use of flank shots to achieve penetration".
- FM 100-5 (1982), p. 7-8 [6]: units should "operate on his flanks and rear,
  where direct fire is most effective".
- *Certain Victory*, p. 2 [14]: Eagle 66's driver steered to follow the gun
  tube. He "kept the 2-foot-thick frontal armor pointed toward the enemy".
  Page 294: tanks at Medina Ridge reversed with their fronts to the enemy.
- GAO NSIAD-92-94, p. 24 [12]: the Abrams that were destroyed "were hit in
  vulnerable locations in the rear or above the turret ring".

**Lineage.** Continues WWI rule 9 (the German 119th Division's advice). The
README found BF1918's hulls armoured alike all round, so the rule bought
nothing there. In 1991 the gap between an M1A1's front and its rear was the
widest any tank had had.

**The rule turns on:** armour by face, for the M1A1 and for the T-72.

### Rule 9. The reverse slope: crest together and fire first

**Rule.** A Soviet-trained defender digs in behind a crest. He means to shoot
each tank as it comes over, one at a time, at his own best range. Come over in
line, already looking, and fire first. If his line is too far back, stay on
the crest and shoot from beyond his reach.

**Sources.**
- FM 100-2-1, *The Soviet Army: Operations and Tactics*, 16 July 1984, pp. 6-3
  to 6-4 [7]: "a reverse slope defense is often chosen". One advantage:
  "Attacking enemy forces will be silhouetted on the crest of the hill." One
  cost: "the maximum range of all weapon systems cannot be exploited".
- FM 100-2-1, p. 6-3 [7]: in a strongpoint, "Vehicles are dug in". Fire is
  planned into "fire sacks", which the manual likens to a US kill zone.
- FM 100-2-1, p. 10-3 [7]: "Tanks firing from defilade positions first engage
  attacking tanks at 2 to 2.5 km". Missiles cover out to 3 km. RPGs fire
  inside 1,000 m.
- FM 71-1 (1988), ch. 3 [1], warns the attacker through Patton's 1944 letter:
  anti-tank weapons are usually on reverse slopes, and tanks cross skylines
  without looking.
- IDA D-1110 [15], Funk in the question period: the Iraqi positions "were
  about 300 meters from the military crest", so a force that "came over the
  hill and fired first" won.
- *Certain Victory*, p. 293 [14], on Medina Ridge: the Iraqi line was too far
  behind the ridge. "His tanks could not reach the American tanks on the
  ridge".
- McMaster [17]: the Iraqi commander at 73 Easting meant to destroy Eagle
  Troop piece by piece as it crossed the crest. His reserve sat on a second
  ridge about 3,000 m further east.
- FM 71-1 gives the same advice to its own infantry, p. 4-19 [1]: "Use reverse
  slopes whenever possible."

**Lineage.** WWI rule 4 seen from the other side. The README's rule 4 cites
Haig on Flesquières in 1917, where guns behind a crest hit tanks as they came
over it. The ground in 1991 was the same. The result reversed because of
rules 1 and 5.

**The rule turns on:** ground (a crest), the range each gun is good to, and
who sees whom first. A duel on flat ground cannot show it.

### Rule 10. The missile outranges the gun but must sit still

**Rule.** A TOW reaches further than any tank gun and does not lose accuracy
with range. But the launcher must stand still and keep its sight on the target
for the whole flight, about 20 seconds at full range. Fire from cover, from
the flank, at the edge of range. If a missile is coming at you, move.

**Sources.**
- FM 71-1 (1988), p. 6-21 [1]: TOW gives accurate fire "out to 3,750 meters".
  Flight time is "approximately 18 seconds to reach maximum range". Of the
  M901: "This is not a system that can 'shoot and scoot.'"
- FM 71-1, p. 6-24 [1]: beyond 2,000 m "TOW missiles are more accurate than
  the tank main gun". Its figure sets the enemy tank's effective range at
  2,000 m. The same page asks for flank shots.
- FM 71-1, p. 3-24 [1]: "TOW missiles have slow flight times". Page 3-33:
  minimum arming distance 65 m.
- GAO NSIAD-92-94, p. 17 [12]: "the TOW takes about 20 seconds to hit its
  target" at 3,750 m, and "the Bradley must remain in a stationary position".
  Crews reported tank kills from 800 to 3,700 m. Without a rangefinder some
  fired at targets out of reach and the missiles fell short.
- FM 23-34 (1994), p. 1-1 [10]: "a minimum range of 65 meters and a maximum
  range of 3,750 meters". Para 2-6: wire guidance limits firing over wide
  water and power lines.
- The Soviet missiles are worse in the same way. FM 100-2-3 (1991) [8] gives
  the Sagger "12.5 seconds to 1,500 meters, and 25 seconds to 3,000 meters",
  and says "Evasive action is effective against it, especially at long range."

**Lineage.** WWI rule 7 said the field gun is the answer to the tank. In 1991
the long-range answer is a missile. It keeps the field gun's weakness (it must
stay put) and adds a new one (the round can be outrun or dodged).

**The rule turns on:** missile speed, range, minimum range, and whether the
launcher must stay still and aimed until the hit.

### Rule 11. The RPG: inside 300 m, from the side; keep moving

**Rule.** For the man with the launcher: hold fire to 300 m, shoot from the
flank, expect to need a second round. For the tank: stay beyond 300 m or keep
moving, show as little hull as possible, and put machine-gun fire on the
smoke.

**Sources.** All from TRADOC Bulletin 3, *Soviet RPG-7 Antitank Grenade
Launcher: Capabilities and Countermeasures*, November 1976 [9].
- p. 14, the Soviet rule: best fire is at 300 m, and "The sighting range of
  fire is 500 meters."
- p. 15, US tests: an exposed tank at 300 m has "less than a 30% chance of
  being hit with the 1st round".
- p. 17: "A second round will probably hit, even if the first one missed."
  The advice to crews on the same page is to suppress, use smoke and keep
  moving.
- p. 27: tank-killer teams are "located on the flank of a defending company".
- p. 28: RPG gunners "hold their fire until the attacking unit closes to about
  300 meters".
- p. 29, weaknesses: "Hard to hit a moving target." Reload and re-aim takes 14
  seconds.
- FM 100-2-3 (1991) [8] repeats it: "Reloading and reaiming the RPG-7V
  requires a minimum of 14 seconds."
- For the US soldier, FM 71-1, p. 3-33 [1]: "The LAW is the best infantry
  antitank weapon at ranges less than 100 meters".
- 1991 evidence: IDA D-1110, p. I-32 [15], has a 3rd Armored Division tank
  commander on tape saying RPGs left only scratches on his company's tanks.

**Lineage.** Continues WWI rules 10 and 11 (bundled charges and the anti-tank
rifle: close or not at all) and WWI rule 1 (keep moving under the guns).

**The rule turns on:** rocket speed and drop, reload time, damage by face, and
how far a tank drives in the rocket's flight.

### Rule 12. Aircraft outrange armour; the ZSU is the answer

**Rule.** A helicopter fires from behind cover at a range no tank on the
ground can answer. The gun that can reach it is the ZSU-23-4, good to 2.5 km.
So the helicopter stands off, and the tanks shoot the ZSU first to let the
aircraft work. A jet that comes low enough to use its gun is inside the ZSU's
reach (our reading of the ranges in Part 3 and of the A-10 lost at Medina
Ridge).

**Sources.**
- FM 1-112, *Attack Helicopter Battalion*, 21 February 1991, p. 3-11 [11]:
  crews pick "firing positions that provide standoff ranges and good fields of
  fire". Ch. 4, p. 4-26: the Hellfire section closes "Once the air defense
  threat has been suppressed".
- DoD report, Appendix T, p. T-18 [13]: the AH-64 "had the ability to fight
  from several miles distance".
- FM 100-2-1 (1984), p. 11-9 [7]: "The Soviets consider armed helicopters to
  be a serious threat". Page 11-3: the ZSU-23-4 "has a relatively small
  engagement envelope".
- FM 100-2-3 (1991) [8]: the ZSU has "an effective AA range of 2,500 meters".
- ARI 1532, pp. 30-31 [3]: a ZSU is a least dangerous target to a tank. A unit
  can raise its priority for the aircraft's sake. Then "the crew is expected
  to shoot the ZSU-23/4".
- FM 71-1 (1988), ch. 2, "Engagement Priorities" [1]: Bradleys take BMPs and
  ZSUs; its example gives one platoon "ZSU 23-4s then BMPs".
- *Certain Victory*, p. 298 [14], Medina Ridge: "One ZSU 23-4 managed to shoot
  down an American A-10 aircraft." Tank fire then killed the ZSU. Page 369:
  the A-10's 30 mm gun was deadly when the aircraft came low, but "CAS seldom
  descended below 10,000 feet".
- DoD report, Appendix T, p. T-11 [13]: the A-10 is "susceptible to
  antiaircraft artillery and SAMs".

**Lineage.** New. Nothing in the 1917-18 tank instructions covers it.

**The rule turns on:** the missile's range against the ZSU's, and the damage
of a 23 mm shell on an airframe and of a 30 mm shell on a tank's roof and rear.

### The other side's book

The Iraqi Republican Guard fought to a Soviet pattern mixed with British
habits (Funk's words in [15]). What FM 100-2-1 (1984) [7] says that defence
should look like:

- Company strongpoints 500 to 1,000 m wide, everything dug in (p. 6-3 and the
  battalion section of ch. 6).
- Fire sacks: obstacles and fire planned to trap an attacker (p. 6-3).
- Reverse slopes where the defence is set up in contact (pp. 6-3 to 6-4).
- Engagement by range band: missiles to 3 km, tanks at 2 to 2.5 km, anti-tank
  guns at 1.5 km, RPGs inside 1 km (p. 10-3).
- A regiment's air defence battery has four ZSU-23-4 and four SA-9. The ZSUs
  work in pairs within about 1,500 m of each other (p. 11-3).
- A BRDM-2 missile vehicle carries six Saggers ready, or five Spandrels
  (p. 10-1).

What the US Army found instead in 1991 [15]: positions spread out with little
mutual support, reverse slopes set at the wrong distance, hull angles all over
the compass [16, p. 13], crews in bunkers beside their tanks when the attack
came [16, p. A-8], and no sign of training since August 1990.

## Part 2. The fights

### 2.1 Battle of 73 Easting, 26 February 1991

The best documented small tank fight of the war. DARPA and the Army measured
it for a simulation within months [15][16].

**Who.**
- US: 2nd Armored Cavalry Regiment (Col. Don Holder), leading VII Corps. Three
  troops fought: Ghost (Capt. Joe Sartiano), Eagle (Capt. H.R. McMaster) and
  Iron (Capt. Dan Miller), north to south [15][16].
- The three troops together: 28 M1A1 and 37 Bradleys [16, p. 10]. Eagle Troop:
  10 M1A1 (one carried the squadron's operations officer), 12 Bradleys and two
  4.2-inch mortars [16, p. 10]. McMaster fought nine tanks in a wedge with his
  own in the centre [17][14, p. 1].
- Iraq: the 18th Brigade of the Tawakalna Division, Republican Guard, on its
  own training ground [15][16, p. 8]. IDA's count of its vehicles: 55 T-72,
  3 T-55, 33 BMP-1, 7 ZSU-23-4, 2 SA-13, 12 towed 152 mm guns [16, p. 11].
  That is 91 armoured fighting vehicles against 65. The BMP-1s carried no
  Saggers [16, p. 11]. IDA modelled 19 RPG-7 teams among about 147 dismounted
  men [16, p. 12].

**Weather and sight.**
- Wind about 40 mph, blowing sand and rain. No aircraft could fly
  [16, p. A-6].
- IDA P-2770, p. 13 [16]: optical visibility 400 to 600 m, at times 100 m.
  "Thermal visibility, on the other hand, was as great as 1500 meters or more."
- Other figures for the same hour: 600 m opening to 1,000 m (Bloedorn [15]);
  900 m [14, p. 1]; "less than 1,000 meters" [14, p. 261]; about 200 m
  (McMaster [17]); 400 m (Wikipedia, secondary [20]).
- The M1's fire control corrected for the crosswind by itself (Bloedorn,
  p. I-121 [15]).

**Timeline.**
- 1530: the three troops move east on a 15 km front (Bloedorn [15]). Average
  speed about 18 km/h [16, p. A-6].
- 1605: Ghost and Iron meet Iraqi security elements [16, p. A-7].
- 1607: Eagle's first contact, a Bradley on an Iraqi bunker [17].
- About 1615: Eagle takes fire from men in the buildings of a training camp
  ("the village"). Its nine tanks fire high explosive into it together
  [16, p. A-7][17].
- 1618: Eagle 66 tops a low rise and sees eight T-72s behind berms
  [14, p. 2]. The crew fires three rounds in seven seconds for three kills
  [14, pp. 2-4][15, p. I-141]:
  1. HEAT, laser range 1,420 m, tank moving at 20 mph.
  2. Sabot, range 600 m, through the berm and the tank.
  3. Sabot, range 400 m.
- The other tanks come up on line. The remaining five T-72s burn within ten
  seconds [14, p. 4]. The Iraqi tanks get off two wild rounds [17].
- Eagle reaches the 70 Easting, its limit of advance. McMaster does not stop:
  "Tell them we can't stop." [17]
- Eagle drives on through the brigade's depth to high ground near the 74
  Easting (secondary [20]). The action lasts 23 minutes [17][14, p. 4].
- About 1630 onward: Ghost fights from a ridge at the 73 Easting against
  counter-attacks until late evening. A Bradley, Ghost 16, is hit twice by a
  73 mm gun at 1648. The first round fails. The second kills the gunner
  [15][20].
- IDA P-2770, p. A-7 [16]: the main fight lasted 90 minutes and firing went on
  for six hours. "The first few minutes, however, appear to have been
  decisive."

**Who fired first.** Iraqi infantry fired first, from the buildings. In the
tank fight Eagle 66 fired first. Three T-72s were dead before the others
traversed [14, p. 4].

**Ranges.**
- Eagle 66's first three shots: 1,420 m, 600 m, 400 m [14, pp. 3-4].
- The squadron's first tank shots at the outpost near the 70 Easting: about
  2,400 m, a mile and a half [14, p. 261].
- TOW shots at over 2,000 m, and 25 mm fire to 1,800 m, with 600 m of
  visibility to the eye (Bloedorn [15]).
- A Bradley platoon (Lt. Hains) opened on dug-in T-72s at 300 m with TOW. Its
  first missile's motor failed 10 feet out. The second hit [15].
- One long tank kill is given as 3,450 m by one speaker and 3,750 m by
  another in the same proceedings [15]. Unverified which is right.
- A dart fired at over 2,000 m went through four feet of earth and a T-72's
  front, and threw the engine out of the back (Bloedorn, pp. I-149 to I-150
  [15]).

**Ammunition.** The three troops fired about 125 tank rounds and 80 TOWs in the
real battle. IDA's replay needed 477 tank rounds for the same kills because it
lacked the depleted uranium round's data [16, pp. 17-18].

**Results.** Sources count different things.
- Eagle Troop in 23 minutes: 28 tanks, 16 personnel carriers and 30 trucks
  (the regiment's log, via Wikipedia, secondary [20]). McMaster's own total
  for the troop: about fifty T-72s, twenty-five personnel carriers and forty
  trucks [17]. *Certain Victory*: the first eight tanks in half a minute, then
  30 more armoured vehicles [14, p. 4].
- The regiment at 73 Easting: 120 to 150 armoured vehicles, about 80 of them
  T-72s (Maj. Lute, p. I-115 [15]).
- US losses in the three troops: two Bradleys, one of them to friendly fire,
  and no tanks [16, p. A-8]. Eagle Troop had no casualties [17].
- The regiment over the whole war: 6 killed, 17 wounded, 4 Bradleys and 2
  M113s destroyed [15, p. I-115].

**The Iraqi position.** A reverse-slope line east of the ridge behind the
camp, tanks about 1,000 m back from the crest, minefields either side of the
camp, and a reserve of 18 T-72s on a second ridge 3,000 m further east [17].
Tanks stood in berms; IDA also placed 21 empty berms as false targets
[16, p. 12]. Hull angles pointed 20 to 60 degrees off the line of attack
[16, p. 13].

**What decided it.** IDA refought the battle on the Janus model [16]:
- As it was: the model matched the real losses.
- Clear weather: close to a one-for-one exchange (p. 24).
- Thermal sights for both sides: the model "produced a nearly two-to-one
  exchange ratio in favor of Iraqi forces" (p. 27), and about 65 of 67 US
  vehicles were lost.
- Its conclusion, p. 30: "In good weather, the U.S. advantage provided by the
  thermal sights disappeared".
- A footnote on p. 30 adds a third case. Iraqi tanks that stayed hull-down and
  rose only to fire (rule 6) did much better.

### 2.2 Battle of Medina Ridge, 27 February 1991

The largest tank fight of the war, and the opposite kind: a halted long-range
shoot from a crest.

**Who.**
- US: 1st Armored Division (Maj. Gen. Ronald Griffith). The division's front
  was about 350 M1 tanks; the Bradley scouts were pulled back [14, p. 292].
  The main fight was 2nd Brigade (Col. Montgomery Meigs): Task Forces 4-70,
  2-70 and 1-35 Armor [14, p. 292].
- Iraq: 2nd Brigade of the Medina Division and part of the Adnan Division
  [14, p. 292]. Two lines of T-72s and BMPs, dug in, 100 to 150 m between
  vehicles, ringed with anti-aircraft guns [14, p. 298].

**Time and weather.** About 1130 [14, p. 293]. Dark sky and a wet wind. The
DoD report calls it a 45-minute battle for 2nd Brigade [13, p. T-144].
Wikipedia gives over two hours for the whole action (secondary [21]).

**Ranges.**
- Targets showed on thermal sights at 3,000 m, hard to make out [14, p. 293].
- Task Force 4-70 opened at about 2,800 m [14, p. 293].
- "The farthest any had ever fired in training was 2,400 meters." Crews now
  hit T-72s at 3,000 m and beyond [14, p. 293].
- The Iraqi line was a reverse-slope defence set too far from the ridge. Its
  tanks could not reach the ridge. The Americans could just reach them
  [14, p. 293].
- On the right, Task Force 1-35 kept moving at 15 km/h, firing. One company
  closed to 800 m. One tank there killed four T-72s in turn, the first through
  its berm, before any fired [14, p. 294].

**Air and air defence.** Apaches hovered 30 feet above the tank line and fired
Hellfires over it [14, p. 293]. A ZSU-23-4 shot down an A-10. Its tracer gave
it away and tank fire destroyed it at once [14, p. 298]. Iraqi artillery fell
behind the US line and was never corrected [14, p. 293].

**Results.** Sources differ.
- DoD: 2nd Brigade destroyed 100 tanks and more than 30 BMPs [13, p. T-144].
- *Certain Victory*: more than 300 armoured vehicles for the division's day,
  for one US soldier killed [14, p. 300].
- Wikipedia (secondary [21]): 186 tanks and 127 other armoured vehicles; four
  Abrams hit, one lost; 38 Iraqi tanks credited to Apaches and A-10s; one A-10
  and two Apaches shot down. Not checked against its sources.

### 2.3 Others, in short

**Objective Norfolk, night of 26 to 27 February.** 1st Infantry Division
passed through the 2nd Cavalry and attacked at night in rain. It is the war's
lesson in friendly fire. In thermal sights an RPG bursting on a vehicle looked
like a tank gun firing. US tanks then shot their own side [14, p. 285]. The
cost in one brigade: six soldiers dead, and it "destroyed five Abrams and five
Bradleys" [14, p. 285]. OTA's table of incidents has the same event: six
killed and 25 wounded [18, p. 27]. Not a duel to stage. It is the case for
Part 4's point on identification.

**Kuwait International Airport and the "Reveille engagement", 25 to 27
February.** Marine M60A1s and a few M1A1s against T-55s, T-62s and some T-72s
in oil smoke. The story usually told: Bravo Company, 4th Tank Battalion (13
M1A1) met about 35 Iraqi tanks at dawn on 25 February and destroyed them in
under 90 seconds; its longest kill was a BMP at 3,750 m. This comes from
Wikipedia citing the company's own website (secondary [23]). The Marine Corps
history could not be opened. **Unverified.**

**Khafji, 29 January to 1 February.** The first ground fight. Saudi and Qatari
troops with Marine support retook the town. Qatari AMX-30s killed T-55s. Most
of the damage was done from the air (secondary [24]). Its tank lesson is again
friendly fire: four Marines died when a TOW from one LAV hit another
[18, p. 27].

**The Challenger 1 long shot, 26 February.** The National Army Museum says a
Challenger of the Royal Scots Dragoon Guards destroyed an Iraqi tank at about
5 km, the longest tank kill on record [26]. Wikipedia gives 5,100 m with an
L26A1 depleted uranium dart, and a separate 4,700 m hit on a fuel tanker by
another crew (secondary [25]). Its 73 Easting page says 4.7 km against a T-55
(secondary [20]). **The exact range is unverified.** No primary account was
opened.

### 2.5 What a staging needs, for the two best

**73 Easting, Eagle 66's first half-minute.** One M1A1 at the point of a
nine-tank wedge, moving east at about 30 km/h. Eight T-72s in berms on a
reverse slope, crews slow to man their guns, turrets not on the attacker.
Ranges 1,420 m closing to 400 m. HEAT in the gun, sabot next. The eye sees
400 to 900 m; the thermal sight sees 1,500 m or more. The T-72s' guns are set
for 1,800 m and they have no thermal sight. The real result: eight for none in
about thirty seconds, then 23 minutes of driving through the brigade.

**Medina Ridge, the brigade shoot.** A line of M1A1s halted on a crest. Dug-in
T-72s and BMPs in two lines 2,800 to 3,000 m away, beyond their own effective
range. Apaches firing Hellfires from just above the tanks. A ZSU-23-4 in the
Iraqi line that kills a low A-10 and is then killed by a tank. Unadjusted
Iraqi artillery falling behind the crest. A second act on the right flank: a
company that closes to 800 m on the move.

The two fights are the two halves of the card. 73 Easting is rules 1, 3, 5 and
9 at short range. Medina Ridge is rules 4, 6, 9 and 12 at long range.

## Part 4. Where 1991 doctrine depends on what a game may not have

1. **The thermal sight.** This is the largest. Rules 1, 5 and 9 rest on one
   side seeing 1,500 m in a storm that held the other to 400 to 600 m
   [16, p. 13]. IDA's replay says that with equal sights the Iraqi side wins
   73 Easting about two to one, and in clear weather it is about even
   [16, pp. 24, 27, 30]. A game in which both crews see the same distance
   plays that second battle, not the real one.
2. **Night.** FM 100-5 gives the night to the attacker [6, p. 3-1]. A T-72's
   infrared sight reached 800 m [8][19]. A game with no night, or with night
   that blinds both sides alike, has no such gap.
3. **Armour that defeats the enemy's best round from the front.** No Abrams
   was lost to enemy fire [12, p. 24]. Crews reported 125 mm hits that failed,
   though DoD would not confirm them [13, p. T-145]. Rule 8's "front to the
   enemy" was close to immunity. A game that gives each tank hit points and a
   three-to-one front-to-rear ratio lets a T-72 win from the front with
   enough hits.
4. **Laser rangefinder and ballistic computer.** First-round hits at 2,000 to
   3,000 m came from a measured range, a computed lead, and wind and
   temperature sensors [15, p. I-121]. Rule 4's battlesight is the fallback
   when that fails. A game with slow shells and a steep drop makes every shot
   a range guess, which is 1943 gunnery. A game with flat instant shells makes
   every shot a first-round hit for both sides, which removes the M1A1's edge.
5. **A flat, fast round.** Battlesight at 1,200 m works because a 1,600 m/s
   dart rises under a metre on the way [3, p. 53]. At a tenth of that speed
   there is no battlesight zone at all.
6. **The stabilised gun.** Rule 5 is only true if the hull's motion does not
   reach the shell. The 1918 README found that BF1942 adds the hull's speed to
   the shell. If Desert Combat's tanks do the same, the engine sides with the
   1943 manual against the 1991 one.
7. **Wire guidance.** Rule 10 needs a missile that goes where the sight points
   for 18 to 22 seconds and falls if the launcher moves or the gunner is
   hit. A fire-and-forget rocket, or a missile that flies at shell speed,
   removes both the weakness and the counter (suppress the launcher, or dodge).
8. **Minimum range and launch delay.** 65 m and 1.5 s for TOW [10]. 500 m for
   a hand-steered Sagger [8].
9. **Earth that stops HEAT but not a dart.** Rule 3's berm lesson and rule 6's
   "dig down, not up" need two answers from the ground. An engine in which
   ground stops every shell alike makes a berm real armour, which it was not.
10. **Ground at all.** Rules 6 and 9 need a crest. The 1918 card had the same
    gap (its "Open" section, rules 4 and 5).
11. **Telling friend from foe.** Crews could hit at 3,000 m and identify at
    1,500 m [12, p. 33]. A quarter of US dead were killed by their own side
    [18, p. 26]. A game that names every vehicle under the crosshair has no
    such limit. It also has no reason for the tight fire control the manuals
    spend chapters on.
12. **Crews not at their guns.** At 73 Easting many Iraqi crews were in
    bunkers when the first rounds landed [16, p. A-8]. Their guns were set
    for the wrong range [14, p. 261]. A staged duel with an alert enemy who
    aims well is a harder fight than the real one.
13. **Ammunition fires and blow-out panels.** Most T-72s hit burned and threw
    their turrets [15, p. I-54]. The Abrams' blow-out panels and fire
    suppression saved crews [12, p. 24]. Hit points model neither.
14. **Artillery and numbers.** 2,000 howitzer rounds and 12 rockets fell on
    the Iraqi line at 73 Easting [14, p. 262]. A one-against-one school shows
    none of it, and none of rule 7.
